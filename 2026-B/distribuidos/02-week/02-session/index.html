Planning — choosing your architecture, bounded contexts and the ADR

The second session of each week is planning: you turn what you learned into decisions for your product's next sprint. Today you will map your domain into bounded contexts, choose an architecture with a decision path (not by fashion), and record it as an Architecture Decision Record (ADR) — the artifact that lets your team defend "why" months later.

1

Map the domain into bounded contexts (context map).

2

Choose an architecture with an explicit decision path.

3

Write ADR-001 with alternatives and consequences.

4

Turn the decision into backlog items for the sprint.

1. How planning works here

Note

Planning converts learning into committed work. The loop: refine the backlog → decide (architecture, boundaries, contracts) → record the decision (ADR) → slice it into user stories with testable acceptance criteria for the sprint. A decision without an ADR is a rumor; a story without acceptance criteria is a wish.

2. Map the domain: bounded contexts first

Note

Before choosing services, map your domain into bounded contexts — areas with their own language and rules. Draw a context map: the contexts and how they relate (who calls whom, who owns what data). Service boundaries, if any, will follow these lines.

4. Record it: the ADR

Note

An ADR (Architecture Decision Record) is a short file (docs/adr/adr-001-architecture.md) with: Context (the forces), Decision (what you chose), Alternatives considered (and why rejected), Consequences (good and bad). It is immutable once accepted; a change is a new ADR that supersedes it.

# ADR-001: Initial architecture
Status: Accepted   Date: 2026-08
Context: 4 bounded contexts; only Catalog needs independent scale (spikes).
Decision: Modular monolith + extract Catalog as a service.
Alternatives: (a) full microservices - rejected: ops cost too high for our size.
              (b) pure monolith - rejected: Catalog scaling would waste resources.
Consequences: + Catalog scales alone; - one cross-service call to keep consistent.

5. A path you will face in real life

Caution

Scenario — the "let's do 12 microservices" trap. A teammate proposes splitting the product into 12 services on day one "because that's how big companies do it." Your move as a team: refute with evidence — cite that premature decomposition multiplies operational cost (deployment, tracing, versioned contracts, data consistency) with no scaling need yet; propose a modular monolith now and an ADR that lists the trigger (a real, measured scaling need) for each future extraction. Disagreeing with references is exactly the professional stance this course grades.

6. Turn the decision into the sprint backlog

Tip

Slice the decision into user stories with testable acceptance criteria, e.g. "As the team, we want the Catalog module isolated behind a port so that it can later be extracted" — AC: no other module imports Catalog internals; all access goes through the port; tests pass. Estimate, prioritize (MoSCoW), and commit only what fits the sprint.

7. Common mistakes

  • Choosing microservices before there is a boundary or a scaling need.
  • Context map drawn from database tables instead of business language.
  • ADR that states the decision but hides the rejected alternatives.
  • Stories without testable acceptance criteria ("make the architecture better").
