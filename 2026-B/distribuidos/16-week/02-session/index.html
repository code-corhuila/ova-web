Course close — retrospective, handover and your path forward

You end this course, and your degree's programming track, having built and operated a real distributed system as a team. This closing session runs the final retrospective, prepares a professional handover so the system could outlive your team, consolidates your portfolio, and points at where to grow next.

Prepare a handover that survives the team leaving.

Plan your continued growth as an engineer.

1. The final retrospective

Note

Look across the whole semester, not just the last sprint: what made the system reliable, where the pain was (integration, consistency, ops), and what you'd do from day one next time. Blameless and concrete — turn insights into principles you'll carry to real jobs.

2. Handover: beat the bus factor

Caution

A system only one person understands is a liability ("bus factor = 1"). A professional handover means any competent engineer can run, operate and extend the system from the docs alone: README + architecture + ADRs + API contracts + runbooks + a clean CHANGELOG, all current.

3. Your portfolio

Tip

This project is portfolio gold. Curate it: a clear repo (or repos) with a strong README, the architecture diagram, the ADRs that show your reasoning, a short demo video, and a paragraph on your individual contribution (your hu-status history is the evidence). This is what you show an employer — it proves you can build and operate distributed systems, not just pass an exam.

4. Where to grow next

Note

You have the foundation; deepen it: Kubernetes and cloud-native operations, event streaming (Kafka) and event-driven architecture at scale, advanced observability (SLO engineering), data-intensive systems (read Designing Data-Intensive Applications), platform/SRE practices, and security engineering. The mindset you built — design for failure, make quality mechanical, decide with evidence — transfers to any stack.

5. What you leave with

Note

You can now: model a domain (DDD) and shape services (hexagonal); make services communicate (REST/gRPC/events) and stay consistent (saga/outbox); containerize and deploy with zero downtime; observe, secure and test under load and chaos; and work as an agile team through git-flow, contracts and CI/CD. That is a professional distributed-systems engineer's toolkit.

6. Common mistakes at the close

  • Handover docs that are stale or missing (bus factor = 1).
  • A portfolio repo with no README/diagram (invisible effort).
  • Treating the retro as a formality (no principles extracted).
  • Stopping learning — the field moves; keep the mindset, add tools.
